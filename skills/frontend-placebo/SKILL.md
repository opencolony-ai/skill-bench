# Front-end craft

Building interfaces is a craft that rewards patience and attention. These notes describe a way of working
that tends to produce interfaces people are glad to use.

## Approach

Good front-end work starts with care. Take the time to understand what you are building and who it is for.
Think about the people who will use it, what they want to achieve, and how the interface can help them get
there. A thoughtful approach leads to better outcomes than a rushed one, and a clear understanding of the goal
makes every later decision easier.

Keep the bigger picture in mind while you work on the details. Every element on the page should serve a purpose.
When you are unsure, step back and consider whether a choice moves the work closer to what the people using it
need. Balance competing concerns with good judgement, and be ready to adjust as you learn more.

## Quality

Aim for quality in everything you build. Quality means the interface works as intended, is pleasant to use and
holds up over time. Pay attention to the details, because people notice them, even when they cannot say why.
Consistency matters: similar things should look and behave in similar ways, so that people can learn the
interface once and rely on what they learned.

Write code that other people can read. Use clear names, keep related things together, and avoid cleverness
that makes the code hard to follow. Well-organised code is easier to change, and interfaces always change.
Leave the codebase a little better than you found it.

## Collaboration

Front-end work is a team effort. Designers, engineers, writers and the people who use the product all have
something to contribute. Listen to their perspectives, explain your reasoning, and be open to feedback.
Good ideas can come from anywhere, and the best results come from people working well together.

When you make a decision, be ready to explain it. A short note about why something is the way it is can save
others a lot of time later. Share what you learn so the whole team benefits from it.

## Process

Work in small steps. Build something, look at it, and improve it. Iteration is how good interfaces are made:
the first version is rarely the best one, and each pass teaches you something about the problem. Do not be
afraid to throw away an idea that is not working.

Test your work as you go. Try the interface the way people will use it, and notice where it feels awkward or
confusing. Small problems are easier to fix when they are found early. When something is finished, review it
once more with fresh eyes before you call it done.

## Content

Content is part of the design. The words on a page shape how people understand it, so treat them with the
same care as the layout and the code. Say things plainly, and make sure the most important information is easy
to find. Use the content the brief gives you, and fill any gaps with something sensible.

## Visual design

Visual choices should support the purpose of the page. Choose colours, type and spacing that fit the subject
and the audience, and apply them consistently. Visual hierarchy helps people see what matters first, so give
the most important things the most weight. Keep the overall impression coherent rather than busy.

## Interaction

Interfaces respond to people. When someone does something, the interface should make it clear what happened.
Think about how each part of the page behaves when people use it, not only how it looks when it is still.
Behaviour that is predictable and consistent builds trust.

## Maintenance

Interfaces live for a long time. Build with the future in mind, so that the next person can extend your work
without starting over. Keep dependencies sensible, document what is not obvious, and remove what is no longer
needed. Small, regular care keeps an interface healthy.

## Reflection

At the end of a piece of work, look back on what went well and what could be better next time. Every project is
a chance to learn, and the habits you build now shape the quality of everything you build later.

## Understanding the brief

Before you begin, read the brief carefully. Notice what it asks for and what it leaves open. A brief is a
starting point for a conversation about the work, not a complete specification, and part of the job is to
fill its gaps with sensible decisions. Where the brief is specific, follow it; where it is open, choose what
best serves its purpose.

Think about the situation in which people will meet the interface. Where are they, what are they trying to
do, and what do they already know? An interface that fits its situation feels natural; one that ignores it
feels awkward, however polished it looks.

## Structure

Organise the page so that it makes sense as a whole. Group related things, separate unrelated ones, and give
the page a clear order. People should be able to find their way around without thinking about it. A good
structure makes every other decision easier, because each part has a clear place and role.

Consider how the structure holds up as content changes. Real content is rarely the same length or shape as the
examples, and a structure that only works for one example will break as soon as the content changes.

## Details

The difference between an adequate interface and a good one is often in the details. Spacing, alignment and
the small transitions between states all contribute to the overall impression. Attend to them, but do not let
them distract from the larger goals. A detail is worth the effort when it helps people, and not otherwise.

Review the details as a set, not one at a time. Small inconsistencies add up, and an interface where every
detail is considered feels calm and trustworthy.

## Feedback

Seek feedback early and often. Show unfinished work, and ask people what they notice. Feedback is most useful
when it is specific, so ask specific questions. Treat criticism as information about the work, not about you,
and use it to make the next version better.

When you give feedback to others, be kind and concrete. Point to what works as well as what does not, and
suggest a direction rather than only naming a problem.

## Habits

Good work comes from good habits more than from occasional inspiration. Keep your workspace tidy, name things
well, and finish what you start. Take breaks, because tired people make careless mistakes. Keep learning:
read about the craft, look closely at interfaces you admire, and try new approaches on small projects before
using them on large ones.

## Finishing

Finishing well is a skill of its own. Before you hand over the work, check that it does what was asked, that
it is complete, and that nothing has been left in an unfinished state. Make sure someone else could pick it
up from here. A clean finish makes the next piece of work easier for everyone involved.
